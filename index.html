Functional Ear Trainer

Hear where a note sits in the key — not how far it is from the last one.

Why this is different

Naming isolated intervals is hard because two notes in a vacuum give your ear nothing to hold onto. Here a tonic is always present — as a drone or a cadence — so every note has a fixed reference and a recognisable character rather than a measurement.

Start at Level 1 with three notes. Don't move up until you're above 85%. Intervals become easy later, as a side effect.

How to actually practise this

Ten minutes a day, not an hour a week. This is a memory task, and memory responds to frequency far more than duration. Ten minutes daily will beat any amount of weekend cramming.

A session looks like this

1. Match the pitch for one minute — no naming, just sing back what you hear. This wakes up the link between ear and voice.
2. Hear → sing → name for six or seven minutes at your current level.
3. Sing a named degree for two minutes. This is the direction almost everyone neglects, and it is the one that fixes "I can hear it but can't name it".

Rules that matter

Sing every answer, even in Identify mode. Hum it under your breath if you must. Producing the pitch is what builds the memory; clicking alone builds very little.
Stay at a level until you are above 85%. Moving up early is the single most common way this goes wrong, and it feels like failure when it is only impatience.
When you get one wrong, listen to the whole correction. The comparison it plays — your answer, then the real one, each against do — is where the learning happens. Skipping it wastes the mistake.
Say the character, not just the number. "The leaning one" sticks; "degree 7" does not.

What progress looks like

Level 1 should come quickly — do, mi and sol are very distinct. Level 3 is where it gets real, because fa and ti are the two people confuse, and they are also the two with the strongest pull. If you stall, that is expected, not a sign this is not working. Drop back a level for a few days and come back to it.
